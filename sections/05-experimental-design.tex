\section{Experimental Design}
\label{sec:design}

\subsection{Network Conditions}
\label{sec:design-network}

Four slices share one origin, one renderer and one encoding of each
sequence, \gls{ldp} \gof{}=4, and differ only in the impairment
applied at the origin's egress (Table~\ref{tab:impairments}). Holding
the encoder fixed across slices, which the three-tier study
of~\cite{mosaic2026} did not, makes Slice~A, with no added loss or delay, a valid reference
for B, C and D, and it is the slice from which the ceilings $\qc$ of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ceiling}, the clean transfer time $\tau_{\mathrm{dl}}$
and the packet counts $N_p$ of Section~\ref{sec:model-layer1} are
measured. Slice~B applies loss only and Slice~C delay only, each at a
level that produces a graded effect on its own. Slice~D combines a
delay and a loss level that are each \emph{null} on their own, so that
any effect it produces is the interaction of Eq.~\eqref{eq:pslow} and
not the sum of two single-factor effects. The design therefore tests
Claim~1 across three impaired slices whose misses have three different
transport origins, loss, delay and their interaction, and tests
non-separability at the transport level without requiring a
collapsed combined slice. \todo{Confirm from the campaign scene that
the content path of all twelve conditions (three sequences $\times$
A--D) is the \texttt{\_LD\_gof4\_} encode, Slice~A included.}

\subsubsection{Slice operating points}
\label{sec:calibration}
Each impaired slice is a single operating point on the
server-to-renderer path, applied with \texttt{netem} on the server's
egress. The values were chosen by calibration rather than taken from
a standard. First, the headless transport harness of
Section~\ref{sec:sys-measurement} mapped the null, graded and
collapse regions of each impairment for this client (129 sessions,
verified with qlog and pcap). Then calibration laps in Unity, on the
rendering machine used by participants with its frame rate capped at
\SI{33}{fps}, selected the value in each region that produced a
visible, graded effect. We then position each operating point against
the standardized 5G QoS characteristics of 3GPP TS~23.501,
Table~5.7.4-1~\cite{3gpp23501} (Table~\ref{tab:impairments}).

\emph{Slice~B (loss only)} drops 2.5\% of packets with no added
delay. At LAN round-trip times each loss is recovered within the \gof
that suffered it, so loss alone is null below 1\% in the harness and
becomes visible in playback only at about 12 losses per \gof. At
$2.5 \times 10^{-2}$, B exceeds the loosest packet error rate in the
5QI table ($10^{-2}$, 5QI~1) and therefore lies outside every
standardized QoS class.

\emph{Slice~C (delay only)} adds \SI{60}{\milli\second} of one-way
delay with no loss. Without loss, delay does not reduce throughput; it
only shifts each fetch later. The client's effective fetch budget is
about \SI{90}{\milli\second} per \gof (Eq.~\eqref{eq:budget}), and
the median \gof takes 35 to \SI{39}{\milli\second} to send, which
leaves about 52 to \SI{55}{\milli\second} for network delay. The
harness measured the degradation threshold at 50 to
\SI{55}{\milli\second}. At \SI{60}{\milli\second}, C exceeds the
largest \gls{xr} packet delay budget (\SI{20}{\milli\second}, 5QI~90)
threefold but remains within the \SI{100}{\milli\second} budget of
5QI~7 (live video streaming, interactive gaming).

\emph{Slice~D (combined)} adds \SI{9}{\milli\second} of one-way delay
and 0.05\% loss. Each component alone produces no late \gofs.
Combined, they leave 58 to 90\% of \gofs late. Each loss reduces the
congestion window, and the window grows back only while it limits
sending, which is about \SI{2}{\milli\second} of each
\SI{35}{\milli\second} burst. At \SI{9}{\milli\second} \gls{rtt}, a
reduction is therefore barely recovered before the next \gof, the
decoder's lead drains, and late \gofs arrive in blocks
(Section~\ref{sec:model-drain}). The \SI{9}{\milli\second} delay is
within the budgets of 5QI~88 (\SI{10}{\milli\second}) and 5QI~89/90
(15 and \SI{20}{\milli\second}, visual content for cloud, edge or
split rendering). The $5 \times 10^{-4}$ loss lies between the motion
tracking class ($10^{-3}$, 5QI~87/88) and the split rendering class
($10^{-4}$, 5QI~89/90).

Together, the three slices show that single impairments degrade
delivery only beyond the specification (B beyond every class, C beyond
the \gls{xr} classes), while their combination degrades it within the
\gls{xr} delay class. The loss level needed for a comparable effect
falls by a factor of about 50 between LAN round-trip time and
\SI{9}{\milli\second}.

Two qualifications apply to this mapping. First, a 5QI packet delay
budget covers the path from the UE to the UPF, whereas our impairment
sits on the server-to-renderer path, so the mapping reads as ``if this
path were a 5G QoS flow with these characteristics''. Second, the 5QI
packet error rate counts packets lost after link-layer
retransmission, which makes it comparable to the IP-level loss that
\texttt{netem} applies. The operating points are specific to this
client, since they depend on the \SI{133}{\milli\second} \gof period,
the \SI{33}{fps} frame cap and the Reno congestion control in quic-go,
which is why they were obtained by calibration.

\subsection{Subjective Experiment}
\label{sec:method}

\subsubsection{Protocol}
The study follows ITU-T P.910~\cite{p910} with the \gls{acr} method on
the five-point scale (5~Excellent to 1~Bad); a single-stimulus method
matches how a viewer consumes a stream and needs no reference
presentation inside the headset, unlike the double-stimulus methods of
ITU-R BT.500~\cite{bt500}. The rated attribute is
stated to participants as the \emph{smoothness of playback} of the
figure in front of them, not its visual fidelity; the instruction and
the training clips make the distinction explicit, because the
impairments under test manifest as freezes and frame skips while
spatial quality is fixed within a session
(Section~\ref{sec:model-scope}). Each stimulus is a
\SI{10}{\second} sequence played twice in a row (\SI{20}{\second}),
which exceeds the P.910 recommendation to leave time for \gls{6dof}
exploration without inducing fatigue~\cite{mosaic2026}. A session
opens with a training phase of about \SI{60}{\second} that shows the
three sequences on Slice~A so that the top of the scale is anchored
to the clean presentation, followed by the rated trials: three
sequences $\times$ four slices (A--D) for every participant, twelve
trials, in a Latin-square order balanced across participants
so that no slice systematically follows another; this addresses the
fixed presentation order of~\cite{mosaic2026}. Ratings are given
verbally after each trial and entered by the experimenter, keeping
the participant's hands free and the headset on. A session lasts
about \SI{30}{\minute} including briefing and fitting.

\subsection{Data Processing and Statistical Analysis}
\label{sec:design-analysis}

\subsubsection{Opinion scores}
For each condition (sequence $\times$ slice) we report the mean
opinion score with a bootstrap 95\% \gls{ci} (10,000 resamples over
participants). Because \gls{acr} ratings are ordinal, inference uses a
\gls{clmm} with the rating as ordinal response and participant and
sequence as random intercepts. A first model with slice as a fixed
effect establishes that B, C and D each differ from A. The test of
Claim~1 is a second model with the session's measured $\tlf$ (and
$\phi$) as covariates: if ratings depend on the network only through
presentation timing, adding slice identity to this model yields no
significant improvement (likelihood-ratio test), and sessions with
$\tlf \approx 0$ on impaired slices are not distinguishable from
Slice~A. The freeze extension of Section~\ref{sec:model-layer2} is
the test $\alpha_\phi = 0$ in the same framework.

\subsubsection{Transport telemetry}
Every rated trial is aligned with the client's per-unit log and
session counters for the same session, from which $\tlf$ and $\phi$
are computed as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:tlf}. Slice~A supplies
$\tau_{\mathrm{dl}}$ and $N_p$ per sequence, the server logs supply
$r_0$ and $\kappa$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:recovery}, and the client log
supplies $f$ and $H$. The four Layer~1 predictions of
Table~\ref{tab:predictions} are then checked against the logs: the
pure-delay threshold against the delay scan, $p_{\mathrm{slow}}$
against the logged slow-unit fraction per session, the Monte Carlo
$\tlf$ and its spread against the logged $\tlf$ per session, and the
regime boundary against the sessions that collapsed.

\subsubsection{Model fitting and held-out evaluation}
Layer~2 is fitted on \emph{measured} $\tlf$: with $\qc$ fixed by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ceiling} from Slice~A, $\alpha$ is estimated by
non-linear least squares of Eq.~\eqref{eq:crystal} to the per-session
ratings, and its 95\% \gls{ci} is obtained by bootstrap over
participants. Generalization is tested by leave-one-out: $\alpha$ is
refitted with one slice (or one sequence) withheld and the withheld
condition is predicted without refitting; we report \gls{plcc},
\gls{srocc} and \gls{rmse} on the withheld conditions, pooled. The
same protocol is applied to the regression of Eq.~\eqref{eq:udora},
refitted on our ratings per sequence, and to a separable model in
$(d, P)$, so that the comparison with prior forms is at equal data and
equal held-out protocol rather than against published figures.
