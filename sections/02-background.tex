\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Volumetric Media and Point Clouds}
\label{sec:bg-volumetric}

Volumetric media records the properties of a three-dimensional space
rather than a projection of it, so that a viewer may change viewpoint
with six degrees of freedom: translation along, and rotation about, the
three spatial axes. This freedom of viewpoint is what distinguishes
volumetric content from 360\textdegree{} video and is the basis of
presence in virtual and augmented reality~\cite{qualinet}. Among the
representations in use, the point cloud is the most direct: a set of
unordered 3D points, each carrying attributes such as RGB colour, as
produced by depth cameras and LiDAR or synthesised from multi-view
capture. Dense dynamic point clouds of a human figure, such as the
\gls{mpeg}/8i sequences used throughout this work~\cite{8i}, contain on the
order of $10^6$ points per frame at 30 frames per second and require
gigabits per second uncompressed~\cite{pcstreaming21}.

\subsection{Point Cloud Compression: \vpcc}
\label{sec:bg-codecs}

\gls{mpeg} addresses the volume of dynamic point-cloud data through the
\gls{v3c} standard, ISO/IEC
23090-5~\cite{v3c}, of which Video-based Point Cloud Compression
(\vpcc) is the principal component~\cite{vpccgraziosi}. Where
geometry-based codecs such as \gls{mpeg} \gpcc operate directly on the 3D
structure, \vpcc takes a projection route. The point cloud is
segmented into connected 3D \emph{patches}; each patch is projected
onto a 2D plane; and the projections are packed into an \emph{atlas}
that is encoded as three conventional video streams: a geometry map
carrying depth, an attribute map carrying colour, and a losslessly
coded occupancy map marking the valid pixels. The decoder reverses the
process, back-projecting pixels into 3D using the atlas metadata.
Because the maps are coded by an ordinary 2D codec, \gls{hevc}~\cite{hevc}
in the reference software \gls{tmc2}~\cite{tmc2}, \vpcc is codec-agnostic
and hardware-friendly, and it inherits the video codec's prediction
structure.

That inheritance is what matters for transport. Frames are coded in
groups of frames (\gofs) under one of the \gls{hevc} common test
configurations~\cite{hmctc}. The \gls{ra}
configuration prescribed by the \vpcc common test
conditions~\cite{vpccctc} uses a hierarchical structure with I-, P-
and B-frames and a \gof of 32 frames, maximising temporal compression
at the cost of long inter-frame dependency chains; it suits stable
networks. The \gls{ldp} configuration uses only I- and
P-frames and is recommended for real-time and interactive use; with
\gof sizes of 16 or 4 frames it shortens the dependency chain and
raises bitstream resilience at the cost of compression efficiency.
Whatever the configuration, a \gof must be received in full before
any frame in it can be reconstructed, and in a streaming deployment
each \gof carries a presentation deadline. The \gof is therefore the
natural \emph{delivery unit} of a \vpcc stream, the atomic quantity
that must arrive against a deadline, and it is the structural variable
on which \crystal turns: a smaller \gof means fewer packets at risk per
unit but a tighter deadline budget per unit, and which effect dominates
is an empirical question (H2).

Encoding with \gls{tmc2} is slow because it embeds the \gls{hevc} reference
encoder \gls{hm}; replacing \gls{hm} with x265~\cite{x265} for the geometry and
attribute videos, through a command-line shim that preserves \gls{hm}'s
interface, makes content preparation practical while leaving the
bitstream standard-compliant~\cite{mosaic2026}. Real-time decoding on
the edge uses the optimised native decoder of the 5G-MAG \gls{v3c}
platform~\cite{fivegmag}. Attribute quantisation dominates bitstream
composition for textured human sequences: for the Longdress sequence,
attribute coding accounts for roughly 92\% of the encoded size, with
geometry near 6\% and occupancy near 1\%~\cite{mosaic2026}, which
bears on how a stall in delivery is perceived.

\subsection{Quality Assessment of Point Clouds}
\label{sec:bg-qa}

\paragraph{Objective metrics.}
Objective metrics are classified by the reference they require
(full-, reduced- or no-reference) and by whether they operate on the 3D
model or on 2D projections of it. Model-based metrics are independent
of rendering and viewpoint and generalise well across distortions;
projection-based metrics capture rendering effects but depend on camera
selection and pooling. \pcqm~\cite{pcqm}, a full-reference model-based
metric built on local statistics of luminance, chrominance and hue, is
among the most stable predictors of subjective quality across codecs in
recent benchmarks~\cite{ak2024basics}. In this work \pcqm is used only as
a \emph{stimulus descriptor}, to report the intrinsic fidelity of each
encoded sequence; it does not enter the model, because the perceptual
attribute rated in our study is temporal smoothness, on which spatial
fidelity has no bearing.

\paragraph{Subjective assessment.}
Subjective studies provide the ground truth that objective metrics
approximate. For volumetric content the design space is wider than for
2D video: the content may be inspected passively along a fixed camera
path, which is reproducible, or interactively, which is realistic but
introduces inter-viewer variability; it may be shown on a monitor or in
a head-mounted display; and the rated attribute must be stated
explicitly, since a \gls{6dof} scene can be judged on fidelity, smoothness,
presence or comfort~\cite{Alexiou2023}. Two ITU recommendations govern
the protocol. ITU-R BT.500~\cite{bt500} is the broadcast standard: a
strictly controlled laboratory, double-stimulus methods (\gls{dsis}, \gls{dscqs}) in
which the assessor compares a reference with a processed version, and
an impairment scale. ITU-T P.910~\cite{p910} targets multimedia
applications on any device: single-stimulus methods, in particular
\gls{acr} on a five-point scale from ``Bad'' to
``Excellent'', and flexibility over the viewing environment. \gls{acr} matches
how a viewer consumes a stream, needs no reference presentation inside
the headset, and is the protocol adopted here, with BT.500's observer
screening retained. Neither recommendation yet addresses immersive
volumetric content specifically; the methodology of
Section~\ref{sec:method} follows P.910 and the emerging practice for
point-cloud video~\cite{nguyen2023,Cox2023}.

\subsection{Transport: Reliable \quic for Volumetric Delivery}
\label{sec:bg-quic}

A \vpcc stream reaches the renderer as a sequence of \gof chunks, each
fetched as an HTTP object against its own presentation deadline. The
transport therefore matters at the granularity of the chunk: what
happens to one chunk when a packet is lost or delayed, and whether
that disturbance propagates to the chunks behind it. Over
HTTP/1.1 and HTTP/2 the answer is dictated by \gls{tcp}: a single lost
segment holds back every byte queued behind it on the connection
(\gls{hol} blocking), and the multiplexing that HTTP/2 added at the
application layer cannot undo an in-order guarantee imposed below it.
\quic~\cite{rfc9000} was designed to remove that coupling. It carries
multiplexed, independently ordered streams over \gls{udp} in user
space, so loss on one stream does not stall another; it folds
\gls{tls}~1.3 into a 1-\gls{rtt} handshake (0-\gls{rtt} on resumption);
and it identifies a connection by an ID rather than a 4-tuple, so a
session survives an address change. \quic is the substrate of
\gls{http3}, which by August 2025 was served by roughly 35\% of public
web sites~\cite{w3techs2025}, and it is the transport a deployed
volumetric service would most plausibly use: production CDNs, browsers
and mobile stacks already ship it, and it needs no change to the
HTTP-object model that a chunked \vpcc stream fits naturally.

Two properties of standard \quic determine how network impairment
reaches a volumetric decoder. First, \quic is \emph{reliable}: lost
packets are detected and retransmitted, and stream data is delivered
in order. Loss is detected either by acknowledgement (a packet is
declared lost when later packets are acknowledged beyond a reordering
threshold, typically three) or by a \gls{pto} derived from
round-trip estimates,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{\gls{pto}} = \mathrm{sRTT} + \max(4\,\mathrm{\gls{rtt}}_{\mathrm{var}},
  k_{\mathrm{granularity}}) + \mathrm{max\_ack\_delay},
  \label{eq:pto}
\end{equation}
so that the recovery cost of a loss is at least one additional \gls{rtt} and,
when a tail packet is lost, a full \gls{pto}. Second, independence between
streams does not help a \vpcc \gof: all packets of a \gof must arrive
before the \gof can be decoded, so a single late packet delays the whole
delivery unit. The combination converts packet loss into
\emph{delay}, and delay into \emph{stalls} whenever the recovered \gof
misses its playout deadline. Packet-level measurement on the testbed
of Section~\ref{sec:system} shows the two impairments leave distinct
signatures on the per-request delivery-time distribution: added
latency shifts the whole distribution, while loss leaves the median
comparatively intact and expands the tail, the P95/P50 ratio rising
from 1.23 to 4.52 at 5\% loss, because retransmission delay compounds
with congestion-window reduction that persists into subsequent
transfers~\cite{vquest2026}. For \vpcc, the per-\gof deadline-miss
rate observed at the renderer correlates with subjective quality
($\rho_{\mathrm{Spearman}} = -0.34$, $p < 10^{-3}$) but only
partially, because a missed \gof of 4 frames and a missed \gof of 16
frames disrupt different durations of content~\cite{mosaic2026};
this is precisely the gap between a network observable and a
perceptual variable that $\tlf$ is defined to close. Equation~\eqref{eq:pto} and the \gof size
are the two quantities from which Layer~1 of \crystal derives the
temporal loss fraction. Partially reliable \quic
variants~\cite{ravuri2023,haseeb2025} and datagram-based transports
such as \gls{webrtc} change this behaviour by allowing stale data to be
dropped; they are discussed in Section~\ref{sec:related} but are
outside the regime modelled here.
