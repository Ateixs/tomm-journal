\section{System Implementation}
\label{sec:system}

The experiments run on the \mosaic pipeline~\cite{mosaic2026}, rebuilt
for this study. Figure~\ref{fig:architecture} shows the complete
pipeline in four lanes, from content preparation to the headset, and
marks every component as new, as an upstream component we patched, or
as upstream software used unmodified; the Online
Appendix\ifappendix{} (Table~\ref{tab:adaptations})\fi{} lists the
upstream baseline of each component, the change made and its reason. Open reference software was reused
wherever possible and changed only where a hard constraint forced it.
Stage~1 carries \vpcc content from the origin to the edge renderer
over \quic and is the only stage that is impaired; Stage~2 casts the
rendered stereo frames to the headset over a private \fiveg network and
is identical across conditions.

\subsection{Content Preparation}
\label{sec:sys-content}

\subsubsection{Source content}
Three sequences of the 8i Voxelized Full Bodies dataset~\cite{8i},
Category~2 of the \vpcc common test conditions~\cite{vpccctc}, are used
(Figure~\ref{fig:content}): Soldier and Longdress at 10-bit voxel
precision (vox10, $\approx$1.08\,M and 0.85\,M points per frame) and
ThaiDancer at 12-bit precision (vox12, $\approx$3.13\,M). Each has 300
frames at \SI{30}{fps}.

\subsubsection{Encoder back end}
Content is encoded with \gls{tmc2} v25.0~\cite{tmc2} built with its
external-codec path. In this mode \gls{tmc2} links no video encoder:
for each of the three 2D sub-streams (occupancy, geometry, attribute)
it writes a YUV file, calls an external executable with an
\gls{hm}-style command line, and reads back the \gls{hevc} bitstream
and the reconstructed YUV that later encoding stages depend on. With
\gls{hm}~16.20+SCM~8.8 behind this interface, video coding dominates
the encoding time of dense sequences. We point the geometry and
attribute paths to a shim that keeps the \gls{hm} interface and
replaces the encoder behind it\ifappendix{} (Online Appendix,
Figure~\ref{fig:contrib-encoder})\fi. The shim parses the \gls{hm}-style
arguments, selects the \gls{ldp} or \gls{ra} profile from the name of
the configuration file, encodes with FFmpeg~6.1.1 and
libx265~3.5~\cite{x265} at the requested QP (\texttt{bframes=0},
\texttt{tune=zerolatency}, \texttt{preset=fast} for \gls{ldp};
\texttt{bframes=15}, \texttt{preset=medium} for \gls{ra}), and decodes
the result back to YUV where \gls{tmc2} expects the \gls{hm}
reconstruction. \gls{tmc2} is unmodified and cannot tell that the
encoder was replaced. The occupancy map stays on \gls{hm}: it is coded
losslessly, is small, and benefits more from bit-exact reference
behavior than from speed. One profile then encodes in
\SIrange{25}{30}{\minute} for a vox10 sequence and
\SIrange{110}{120}{\minute} for ThaiDancer on a single workstation, and
because FFmpeg and libx265 are the versions packaged with Ubuntu~24.04
LTS, a clean installation reproduces the encoder exactly.

\subsubsection{Fixed atlas size}
The synthesizer of the decoder (Section~\ref{sec:sys-edge}) does not
support a change of atlas size within a session: when a \gof arrives
with different dimensions, the synthesizers are released about
\SI{26}{\milli\second} after the second canvas reconfiguration and never
reinitialize, so only the first \gof renders and the frame freezes
without an error. Unconstrained, \gls{tmc2} sizes the vox10 atlases per
\gof between 1280$\times$1280 and 1280$\times$1536. We therefore fix the
atlas at encoding time with \texttt{--minimumImageWidth} and
\texttt{--minimumImageHeight}: 1280$\times$1536 for both vox10
sequences (1280$\times$1600 for the Longdress \gls{ra} encode) and
2560$\times$4096 for ThaiDancer. The padding costs some bitrate, which
is included in the curves of Figure~\ref{fig:rd}.

\subsubsection{Encoding campaign}
We encode each sequence in three configurations that differ in
prediction structure and \gof length: \gls{ra} with hierarchical B
frames and a \gof of 32, as prescribed by the \vpcc common test
conditions~\cite{vpccctc}, and \gls{ldp} with I and P frames
only~\cite{hmctc} and \gofs of 16 and 4. Geometry QP (16) and occupancy
precision (2) are fixed and the attribute QP is swept
(Figure~\ref{fig:rd}). The gap between sequences at a fixed bitrate
(about \SI{4}{dB}) is an order of magnitude larger than the gap between
configurations of one sequence (\SIrange{0.3}{0.6}{dB}), so differences
between contents cannot be removed by encoder tuning, and \gls{ra} lies
on or just above the \gls{ldp} curves, so short \gls{ldp} \gofs trade
coding efficiency for resilience to delay rather than for fidelity. The
selected attribute QPs (Table~\ref{tab:encoder}) give a
\gls{psnr}$_Y$ of 34--\SI{39}{dB} and bring the three sequences into a
comparable bitrate range. All rated conditions of this study use the
\gls{ldp} \gof{}=4 encodes, whose bitrates are 38.8, 41.1 and
\SI{38.2}{Mb/s} for Soldier, Longdress and ThaiDancer; the \gls{ra} and
\gls{ldp} \gof{}=16 encodes served the three-tier study
of~\cite{mosaic2026} and the rate--distortion characterization. Attribute data dominate the bitstream (for Longdress about
92\% of the size, geometry 6\%, occupancy 1\%). \pcqm~\cite{pcqm} is
computed per sequence as a stimulus descriptor only
(Section~\ref{sec:model-scope}).

\subsubsection{Segmentation}
We split each bitstream with the VpccChunkCreator tool of the 5G-MAG
\textsc{rt-v3c} suite~\cite{fivegmag} at \gof boundaries into one
file per \gof: 75 segments of \SI{133.3}{\milli\second} at \gof{}=4
(about 620--\SI{680}{kB} at the median), 19 of \SI{533}{\milli\second}
at \gof{}=16 and 10 of \SI{1067}{\milli\second} at \gof{}=32. Each
segment is the unit of delivery, of deadline enforcement and of loss,
the delivery unit $k$ of Section~\ref{sec:model-layer1}: the client
presents a \gof completely or not at all.

\subsection{Volumetric Origin and Stage-1 Impairment}
\label{sec:sys-stage1}

\subsubsection{Origin server}
ISO/IEC 23090-10 carries \vpcc as time-bounded HTTP
objects~\cite{v3ccarriage}; we keep this object model and
fetch each \gof over \gls{http3}, which avoids the cross-object
head-of-line blocking of \gls{tcp} and is the transport a deployed
service would most plausibly use (40.9\% of public web sites advertised
\gls{http3} in October 2026~\cite{w3techs2026}). We serve the segments as
static files with Caddy~2.11.1 on an Ubuntu~24.04 virtual machine,
over \gls{http3} with \gls{tls}~1.3. Its \quic stack, quic-go~0.59, uses
a NewReno-style congestion controller with a multiplicative decrease of
0.7 and an initial window of 32 packets. We run one Caddy instance that listens on
one \gls{udp} port per slice, so all slices share the origin, content
and \gls{tls} configuration and differ only in the impairment of their
port. The client issues one GET per \gof; Caddy writes each body in
\SI{32}{KiB} chunks, each leaving as about 24 packets with up to
\SI{1419}{B} of \quic payload, so a \gof{}=4 segment spans 486, 517
and 471 packets at the median for Soldier, Longdress and ThaiDancer and
is sent in about 35--\SI{39}{\milli\second} on the clean slice.

\subsubsection{Impairment chain}
We apply impairment on the egress of the server interface (MTU
\SI{1450}{B}), so only the server-to-renderer direction is impaired;
requests and acknowledgments travel unimpaired. An HTB root classifies
packets by \gls{udp} source port into one class per slice (5001 clean,
A; 5002 loss only, B; 443 delay only, C; 5003 delay and loss, D), and
each impaired class chains a token bucket filter and a \texttt{netem}
queue that applies the slice's loss probability $p$, its one-way delay
$\ell$, or both. Delay is constant and without jitter, so
\texttt{netem} does not reorder packets. One script holds all four chains and resets their counters, and after
every measurement lap we compare the \texttt{netem} drop counters with
the configured loss.

\subsubsection{Transport confounds}
\label{sec:sys-confounds}
Before calibration we found and removed six effects that produced loss
or delay we had not configured, each of which, on its own, created a
loss cliff that looked like a property of \vpcc over \quic. Two were
spurious losses: generic segmentation offload in quic-go and the
twelve transmit queues of the virtual interface each reordered packets
within a flow, and because the client acknowledges every packet at
once, the server's reordering threshold declared the late packets lost
and cut its congestion window by 0.7 each time; in a \SI{30}{\second}
session 364 of 367 declared losses were spurious, and the window
stayed near \SI{30}{kB} instead of growing past \SI{1}{MB}. Three were
in the impairment chain itself: a \texttt{netem} queue without a
rate-limited parent dropped about seven times fewer packets than
configured, a token bucket rounded below one packet rate-limited a
third of the packets on the delay chains, and the default queue
overflowed at \SI{20}{\milli\second} of delay. The sixth was the path:
a VPN that had fallen back to \gls{tcp} added a 99th-percentile
round-trip time of \SI{33}{\milli\second} under load. We disabled
offload, pinned one transmit queue, gave each \texttt{netem} queue a
token-bucket parent with a burst above one packet and a larger limit,
and moved the renderer onto an isolated VLAN with a 90th-percentile
round-trip time of 1--\SI{2}{\milli\second}. After these corrections,
the only packets declared lost on slices A and C are path-MTU probes,
the losses on slices B and D match the \texttt{netem} drop counters
(1.005\% measured for 1\% configured), and every lap is preceded by 60
probes that must arrive without loss within \SI{5}{\milli\second}.

\subsubsection{Server-side measurement}
During calibration laps quic-go writes one qlog trace per connection
and \texttt{tcpdump} records every datagram after the queueing
disciplines. Because \quic encrypts its stream framing, neither source
alone attributes a datagram to a \gof; matching qlog sent-packet events
to captured datagrams by order and size pairs 99.997\% of the packets,
and the joined trace gives, per \gof, the request time, first and last
transmission, the acknowledgment time of every packet, the packet
count, the retransmitted bytes and the congestion window. qlog is
disabled during participant sessions.

\subsection{Edge Renderer}
\label{sec:sys-edge}

The edge renderer is a Windows~11 workstation with an NVIDIA RTX~4070
running Unity~6. The 5G-MAG decoder decodes \gls{hevc} with NVIDIA
CUVID and hands its output over through OpenGL, so Unity must run with
the OpenGLCore graphics API. This single requirement shapes the
renderer lane of Figure~\ref{fig:architecture}: transport, decoder
input and \gls{vr} output were each rebuilt around a decoder that only
speaks OpenGL. Diagrams of the four components are in the Online
Appendix\ifappendix{} (Figures~\ref{fig:contrib-plugin},
\ref{fig:contrib-decoder} and~\ref{fig:contrib-gldx})\fi.

\subsubsection{Components}
\label{sec:sys-client}
\label{sec:sys-scheduler}
\label{sec:sys-decoder}
\label{sec:sys-gldx}
\emph{HTTP/3 client.} A native plugin built on lsquic, with BoringSSL
linked statically, opens one \quic connection per session and sends
each \gof request on a new bidirectional stream; requests return at
once and are polled once per Unity frame. A body shorter than its
announced length is reported as an error, so a truncated \gof never
reaches the decoder, and a cancel sends \texttt{STOP\_SENDING} while
the connection and its congestion window survive. Cancellation is what
makes graded impairment possible: our first client, one \texttt{curl}
process per \gof, could not abort a late download, so abandoned
transfers consumed about two thirds of the bandwidth and any loss
turned into collapse. The plugin also exports the connection's
\gls{rtt}, congestion window and loss counters.

\emph{Deadline scheduler.} Playout runs on a content clock that is
anchored at the first push and never paused, so the player does not
rebuffer (Figure~\ref{fig:contrib-scheduler}). With
$D = \SI{133.3}{\milli\second}$ the \gof period, \gof~$n$ is due at
$E_n = T_a + nD$. Per slot the scheduler keeps one request in flight,
aborts it at $E_n + D$, waits while the decoder input holds two or more
\gofs, and classifies a completed \gof by its lag: up to
\SI{33}{\milli\second} is on time, up to \SI{133}{\milli\second} is late
but pushed, beyond that it is dropped and the scheduler skips ahead to
the \gof whose slot contains the current content time. One request in
flight maps each \gof's delivery time directly onto its deadline; with
four in flight the client ran about \SI{600}{\milli\second} ahead and
hid the network. Timestamps are quantized to the Unity frame
(\SI{30.3}{\milli\second}), so a fetch must finish within three frames
of the \SI{133}{\milli\second} slot (Eq.~\eqref{eq:budget}). An
integrity check walks the \gls{v3c} units of every body before it is
pushed, and the scheduler logs one row per \gof and per-session counts
of on-time, late, lost and skipped \gofs, the counters of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:tlf}.

\emph{Decoder input and teardown.} The 5G-MAG decoder
plugin~\cite{fivegmag} receives segments only through a \gls{dash}
client in a separate DLL. We replaced that DLL with one of the same
name and exports that holds a bounded FIFO: Unity pushes one complete
\gof per call and the decoder thread blocks until one is available, so
\gofs reach the decoder from memory under our deadline policy and the
decoder is unchanged. We modified the decoder itself in one place: a
session that ended after starved delivery froze on teardown because
video threads parked on an empty input never observed the finish flag;
pushing an empty packet per stream before the join, as the audio path
already does, removed the hang, and the fix was reported upstream. The
decoder is restarted between conditions, which permits a fully
randomized presentation order.

\emph{Presentation.} In OpenGLCore, Unity's XR plug-in submits OpenGL
images through OpenXR, which the SteamVR runtime silently rejects. We
disabled every XR loader and wrote a native plugin that shares one
Direct3D~11 texture per eye with OpenGL through
\texttt{WGL\_NV\_DX\_interop2}, copies each eye target into it on the
GPU, and submits both to the OpenVR compositor, reading head and eye
poses back for Unity's cameras. Frames never leave the GPU; the
application frame is capped at \SI{30.3}{\milli\second} to match the
content, and SteamVR reprojects to the panel refresh rate. CUVID and
the interop extension restrict the renderer to NVIDIA GPUs.

\subsection{Stage 2: Edge to Headset}
\label{sec:sys-stage2}

The SteamVR compositor passes the frames to the ALVR streamer, which
encodes them as video for the ALVR client on the Meta~Quest~3; head
tracking returns over the same link. The link is a private \fiveg
standalone network (radio unit, baseband unit, network controller)
with the edge renderer colocated with the gNodeB. The Quest~3 has no
cellular modem, so we attach a commercial 5G USB dongle to its USB-C
port and the headset uses the dongle's interface, never Wi-Fi.
Stage~2 carries rendered frames, not \vpcc content, and is held
constant with identical ALVR settings. It belongs to the
motion-to-photon loop of the headset; Stage~1 does not: an impairment
on Stage~1 decides whether a \gof arrives before its deadline but does
not change the latency of the response to head motion. Every
difference between conditions therefore originates in the Stage~1
transport.

\subsection{Measurement Plane and Headless Harness}
\label{sec:sys-measurement}

Each condition is observed from three vantage points: the server qlog
and capture joined per \gof, the client's per-\gof and per-session logs
with the plugin's transport counters, and the participants' ratings.
Because the Unity Editor needs an operator to start every lap, we
built a headless harness for broad transport scans: a console application that
loads the same plugin DLL (its hash is recorded in every log) and
replays the scheduler, with a virtual decoder that consumes one \gof
every $D$ as the only synthetic element and an option that quantizes
polling to the Unity frame. It writes the same log formats as Unity.
Against a Unity lap recorded on the same day with the same impairment,
its counts of missed slots fell within $\pm$10\% of the 230 slots of a
session and its fetch-time percentiles within one Unity frame. Harness
results select candidate impairment levels only; every perceptual
condition is confirmed in Unity (Section~\ref{sec:calibration}).
